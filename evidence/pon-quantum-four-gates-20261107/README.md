For the classical implementation, exactly one of the 64 pairs of uniform nonce
outcomes gives the legal path `6 -> 1`, hence `(1/8)*(1/8)=1/64`. The seed9 sample
still failed. The statement concerns this fixed initial history and two-attempt

The quantum finite certificate reserves a common distance **47**, **22,253,112
logical patches** and **98,314,690,616 physical qubits**. Under its declared
single-port serial initialization, initialization alone requires
**1045.896264 seconds**. Both q choices therefore stop at initialization within
the four-second model window: zero completed measurement, candidate, proof or
publication. The conditional bound is **[0, epsilon]**, not zero actual risk.
These are uncalibrated construction assumptions; a slow initialization design
does not bound the cheapest legal attacker.

The full/restricted predicate, P=1/2/4, q=0/1/2 grid retains **all 18 cells**, with
18 constructed and 0 refused. Every ideal delivery interval is exactly [0,0];
every conditional upper endpoint is positive. No pair has certified interval
dominance, so all 18 remain undominated. This does not identify an optimal
implementation. Initialization parallelism, factory design, physical layout and
hardware prices are still premises, not an exhaustive architecture search.

[The finite-model note](../../formal/pon-nakamoto-v1/FINITE_QUANTUM_CONSENSUS.md)
contains the theorem, assumptions and proof. For every policy in the declared
finite action class, an independently checked Bellman supersolution `V` bounds
the ideal replacement probability by `V(initial)`. The state retains every
winner's complete block identity and successor; different nonces do not collapse
into a fictitious common parent.

Under the explicit lifecycle contract, fault events have conditional probability
bounds given the complete prior history, and the real timed execution equals the
ideal process outside their union. If that union is at most `epsilon`, every
fixed policy with ideal probability `p` has an actual probability in
`[max(0,p-epsilon), min(1,p+epsilon)]`. The same uniform coupling bounds the finite
class optimum. Fault independence is not assumed. The contract itself is not a
hardware measurement. An incomplete state graph returns no bound.

The optimizer may use census-derived advice to choose among the declared actions.
It is not a cheapest arbitrary miner and does not allow arbitrary
function-dependent initialization or direct selection of a known winning nonce.
The fixed-q sampler does not inspect the census to select its action. A separately
labelled positive path is an observer-selected existential witness, not the
fixed-seed sample. Source binding is not an external timestamped precommitment.

The large-q probability module uses outward dyadic intervals; the finite fork
module independently uses exact `Fraction` amplitudes for its small-q action
class. Both retain legal winners outside a restricted phase-marked set. They
handle empty/full sets and exact overshoot without turning a small positive
probability into zero.

## Literature and the scope of the argument

[Bailey and Sattath](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.08023v2) describe the mismatch
between cumulative classical work increasing by `c` and ideal Grover search
cost scaling by approximately `sqrt(c)`. Their mechanism requires a much richer
variable-difficulty trajectory and extremely fast fault-tolerant quantum
hardware. The bounded native bridge does not reproduce that long-horizon attack.
Changing signatures or blindly replacing chainwork by square-root weights does
not follow from this experiment.

[Cojocaru, Garay and Song, Section 4.1, equation 10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.03723)
use `nu_D = max_x Pr[f(x)=1]` for the distributional hybrid-query bound. It is not
an arbitrary fixed census's average `M/N`. For a point distribution on a known
function containing a winner, this parameter is one. Function-dependent advice
and preprocessing must be addressed before invoking a black-box hardness result.

[Machine resource estimation](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.07629v1) spans algorithm,
architecture, error correction and device assumptions. The lifecycle fields
implemented here are an auditable contract for such inputs; their presence does
not calibrate those inputs or prove physical feasibility.
